Maneka Gandhi v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (1978) 1 SCC 248＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1978) 1 SCC 248

01 / FACTS

The petitioner’s passport was impounded by the Government under the Passports Act, 1967, in the public interest, without prior reasons or a hearing.

02 / ISSUE

Must a law that restricts personal liberty provide a procedure that is fair, just and reasonable, and how do Articles 14, 19 and 21 relate to one another?

03 / REASONING

The Court read Article 21 together with Articles 14 and 19. A “procedure established by law” cannot be arbitrary, unfair or unreasonable, and a law affecting liberty must satisfy each Article that applies.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Fairness is part of Article 21, and arbitrary state action is open to challenge. How the principle works for a given statute still depends on its terms and the facts.

Shreya Singhal v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2015) 5 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2015) 5 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Petitions challenged Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, which punished certain online messages, after arrests over social media posts.

02 / ISSUE

Did Section 66A violate freedom of speech and expression, and was it saved by the reasonable restrictions in Article 19(2)?

03 / REASONING

The Court distinguished discussion and advocacy from incitement. It held Section 66A vague and overbroad, with a chilling effect on speech, and struck it down. It also read down the intermediary takedown rules and upheld Section 69A with safeguards.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Vague criminal laws on speech can be struck down. Online expression receives constitutional protection, subject to restrictions that fit within Article 19(2).

Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2017) 10 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2017) 10 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

A challenge to the Aadhaar scheme led to a reference to a nine-judge Bench on whether the Constitution guarantees a right to privacy.

02 / ISSUE

Is privacy a fundamental right under the Constitution of India?

03 / REASONING

The Bench held that privacy is intrinsic to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and to the freedoms in Part III. It overruled earlier decisions to the extent they said otherwise, and set out that any interference must meet tests of legality, legitimate aim and proportionality.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Privacy is a fundamental right. The decision does not decide every privacy dispute, and later cases apply its tests to specific laws.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of KeralaConstitutional · (1973) 4 SCC 225＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1973) 4 SCC 225

01 / FACTS

The head of a Kerala mutt challenged state land-reform laws and constitutional amendments that limited judicial review of property rights. A 13-judge Bench heard the case.

02 / ISSUE

How far can Parliament amend the Constitution under Article 368?

03 / REASONING

A majority held that Parliament has wide power to amend the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter its basic structure or framework.

04 / TAKEAWAY

The basic structure doctrine. The list of basic features has been developed case by case since.

Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (1980) 3 SCC 625＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1980) 3 SCC 625

01 / FACTS

The petitioners challenged parts of the Constitution (42nd Amendment) Act, 1976, which limited judicial review of amendments and gave Directive Principles priority over certain Fundamental Rights.

02 / ISSUE

Is Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution limited, and can it make that power unlimited by amendment?

03 / REASONING

The Court struck down the challenged clauses. It held that the limited nature of the amending power is itself part of the basic structure, and that the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is essential to the Constitution.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Parliament cannot give itself unlimited amending power. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles must be read in harmony.

S.R. Bommai v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (1994) 3 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1994) 3 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Several state governments were dismissed and President’s Rule imposed under Article 356. In Karnataka, the Chief Minister said he still had a majority in the Assembly.

02 / ISSUE

Is the President’s satisfaction under Article 356 open to judicial review, and on what grounds?

03 / REASONING

A nine-judge Bench held that a proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review. It said majority support should be tested on the floor of the House, and treated secularism as part of the basic structure.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Article 356 is a limited, reviewable power. Federalism and secularism are basic features of the Constitution.

Indra Sawhney v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · 1992 Supp (3) SCC 217＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · 1992 Supp (3) SCC 217

01 / FACTS

A 1990 government order reserved 27% of civil posts for socially and educationally backward classes, following the Mandal Commission report.

02 / ISSUE

Is reservation for backward classes valid under Articles 15(4) and 16(4), and what limits apply?

03 / REASONING

A nine-judge Bench upheld reservation for backward classes. It held that the “creamy layer” must be excluded and that reservation should ordinarily not exceed 50%.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Sets the basic framework for OBC reservation. Later amendments and cases have addressed other questions, so check the current law.

Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2018) 10 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2018) 10 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Petitions challenged Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code to the extent it criminalised consensual sexual conduct between adults of the same sex.

02 / ISSUE

Does Section 377 violate Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 when applied to consensual adult conduct?

03 / REASONING

A five-judge Bench held it unconstitutional to that extent, relying on dignity, autonomy, equality and constitutional morality. It overruled the 2013 decision in Suresh Kumar Koushal.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Constitutional morality, not social morality, guides the Court. Consensual same-sex conduct between adults is not a crime.

Joseph Shine v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2019) 3 SCC 39＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2019) 3 SCC 39

01 / FACTS

A petition challenged Section 497 IPC (adultery) and Section 198(2) CrPC. The offence could be committed only by a man, and the woman could not be prosecuted.

02 / ISSUE

Is the adultery offence consistent with Articles 14, 15 and 21?

03 / REASONING

A five-judge Bench struck down the provisions. It held that they treated a married woman as her husband’s property and denied her autonomy and equal treatment.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Adultery is no longer a criminal offence. It can still be relevant in civil matters such as divorce.

Vishaka v. State of RajasthanConstitutional · (1997) 6 SCC 241＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1997) 6 SCC 241

01 / FACTS

After the gang rape of a social worker in Rajasthan, women’s groups filed a petition about the lack of legal protection against sexual harassment at the workplace.

02 / ISSUE

Can the Court lay down enforceable guidelines to protect working women when no legislation exists?

03 / REASONING

Relying on Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 and on international conventions, the Court issued binding guidelines to prevent and address workplace sexual harassment.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Courts can fill a legislative gap. The guidelines were later replaced by the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.

Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal CorporationConstitutional · (1985) 3 SCC 545＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1985) 3 SCC 545

01 / FACTS

Pavement and slum dwellers in Bombay challenged eviction from the places where they lived and worked.

02 / ISSUE

Is the right to livelihood part of the right to life under Article 21?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that the right to life includes the right to livelihood, because losing one’s livelihood can mean losing the means to live. It added that the right can be restricted by a fair procedure established by law.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Livelihood is protected by Article 21. Eviction must follow a fair procedure, but the case does not create a right to occupy public land.

Bachan Singh v. State of PunjabCriminal · (1980) 2 SCC 684＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1980) 2 SCC 684

01 / FACTS

The accused was convicted of murder and sentenced to death. The case raised the constitutional validity of the death penalty.

02 / ISSUE

Is the death penalty under Section 302 IPC and the related sentencing provision constitutional?

03 / REASONING

A Constitution Bench upheld the death penalty by a majority but held that it should be imposed only in the “rarest of rare” cases, after weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Life imprisonment is the rule and death the exception, decided case by case. Later cases keep refining how the test is applied.

Lalita Kumari v. Government of Uttar PradeshCriminal · (2014) 2 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2014) 2 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

A father said the police did not register an FIR when he reported the kidnapping of his minor daughter.

02 / ISSUE

Must the police register an FIR under Section 154 CrPC whenever information discloses a cognizable offence?

03 / REASONING

A Constitution Bench held that registration is mandatory when the information discloses a cognizable offence, and that no preliminary inquiry is permitted then. A short, time-bound preliminary inquiry is allowed in limited categories of cases.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Police cannot refuse to register an FIR where a cognizable offence is disclosed. Section 154 CrPC is now Section 173 of the BNSS, so check the current text.

Arnesh Kumar v. State of BiharCriminal · (2014) 8 SCC 273＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2014) 8 SCC 273

01 / FACTS

A husband sought anticipatory bail in a case under Section 498A of the Indian Penal Code and the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961. The Court noted a pattern of routine arrests.

02 / ISSUE

Should police arrest automatically in offences punishable with imprisonment up to seven years, or must they first apply statutory conditions for arrest?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that arrest is not a matter of course. Police were directed to apply the arrest criteria under Section 41 of the Code of Criminal Procedure and record reasons, and magistrates were told to check those reasons before authorising detention.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Arrest needs recorded justification. The CrPC has since been replaced by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, so check the corresponding provisions now in force.

Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.Contract · [1893] 1 QB 256＋

COURT OF APPEAL, ENGLAND · [1893] 1 QB 256

01 / FACTS

A company advertised a £100 reward to anyone who caught influenza after using its smoke ball as directed, and said it had deposited £1,000 at a bank. Mrs Carlill used the ball as directed and caught influenza.

02 / ISSUE

Was there a binding contract, or was the advertisement a mere sales “puff”?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that the advertisement was an offer to the whole world, accepted by anyone who performed its conditions. The bank deposit showed serious intention, and notice of acceptance was not required.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Leading case on unilateral offers and acceptance by performance. Section 8 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 reflects the idea.

Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas GhoseContract · (1903) ILR 30 Cal 539＋

PRIVY COUNCIL · (1903) ILR 30 Cal 539

01 / FACTS

A minor mortgaged his property to a moneylender in return for a loan, and later sought to cancel the mortgage.

02 / ISSUE

Is a minor’s agreement void or voidable, and can the lender recover the money?

03 / REASONING

The Privy Council held that a minor’s agreement is void from the start under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, so there is nothing to ratify. The lender could not recover the money from the minor under the restitution provisions.

04 / TAKEAWAY

A minor’s agreement is void in India. The case is a standard reference on capacity to contract.

Hadley v. BaxendaleContract · (1854) 9 Exch 341＋

COURT OF EXCHEQUER, ENGLAND · (1854) 9 Exch 341

01 / FACTS

A mill’s crankshaft broke. A carrier delayed delivering it to the repairers, and the mill owners claimed the profits they lost while the mill stood idle.

02 / ISSUE

How remote can losses be before a party is no longer liable to pay damages for breach?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that damages cover losses arising naturally from the breach, or losses that both parties could reasonably have contemplated when making the contract. The carrier did not know the mill would stop without the shaft.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Sets the remoteness rule for contract damages. Section 73 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 follows a similar approach.

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (Oleum Gas Leak)Tort · (1987) 1 SCC 395＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1987) 1 SCC 395

01 / FACTS

Oleum gas leaked from a fertiliser plant in Delhi, harming people nearby. The Court considered the liability of enterprises that carry on hazardous activities.

02 / ISSUE

What standard of liability applies to an enterprise engaged in an inherently dangerous activity?

03 / REASONING

The Court laid down a rule of absolute liability. Such an enterprise must compensate those harmed, without the exceptions available under the English rule in Rylands v. Fletcher, and compensation can be linked to the enterprise’s size and capacity.

04 / TAKEAWAY

India applies absolute liability for hazardous industries. It is a key case on environmental and industrial accidents.

Donoghue v. StevensonTort · [1932] AC 562＋

HOUSE OF LORDS · [1932] AC 562

01 / FACTS

Mrs Donoghue drank ginger beer from an opaque bottle that allegedly contained a decomposed snail. A friend had bought it, so she had no contract with the manufacturer.

02 / ISSUE

Does a manufacturer owe a duty of care to a consumer with whom it has no contract?

03 / REASONING

By a majority, the House of Lords held that it does. Lord Atkin’s “neighbour principle” says one must take reasonable care to avoid acts that could foreseeably injure people closely affected by them.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Foundation of the modern law of negligence. Indian courts have referred to it when developing tort law.

Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · AIR 1951 SC 458＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · AIR 1951 SC 458

01 / FACTS

The petitioners challenged the Constitution (First) Amendment Act, 1951, which restricted property rights to allow land reform, arguing that it violated Fundamental Rights.

02 / ISSUE

Does “law” in Article 13 include a constitutional amendment, so that an amendment can be struck down for violating Part III?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that the power to amend under Article 368 is different from ordinary law-making, and that “law” in Article 13(2) means ordinary law. The amendment was upheld.

04 / TAKEAWAY

An early view that Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights. Later cases, ending with Kesavananda Bharati, limited this power through the basic structure doctrine.

A.K. Gopalan v. State of MadrasConstitutional · AIR 1950 SC 27＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · AIR 1950 SC 27

01 / FACTS

A political leader was detained under the Preventive Detention Act, 1950, and challenged his detention under Articles 13, 19, 21 and 22.

02 / ISSUE

Did the Act violate Fundamental Rights, and what does “procedure established by law” in Article 21 mean?

03 / REASONING

The majority upheld most of the Act. It read “procedure established by law” as the procedure laid down by a valid law, without requiring that it be fair or reasonable, and treated Articles 19, 21 and 22 as separate codes.

04 / TAKEAWAY

A narrow reading of Article 21 that was later displaced by Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India.

ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant ShuklaConstitutional · (1976) 2 SCC 521＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1976) 2 SCC 521

01 / FACTS

During the Emergency of 1975, many people were detained, and a Presidential order suspended the right to move courts to enforce Articles 14, 21 and 22.

02 / ISSUE

Could detainees ask High Courts for habeas corpus to challenge their detention during the Emergency?

03 / REASONING

By a majority of four to one, the Court held that they could not. Justice H.R. Khanna dissented, holding that liberty does not depend on Article 21 alone.

04 / TAKEAWAY

The decision was widely criticised. In Puttaswamy (2017) the Court said it was wrongly decided. The 44th Amendment now prevents suspension of Articles 20 and 21 in an Emergency.

E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil NaduConstitutional · (1974) 4 SCC 3＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1974) 4 SCC 3

01 / FACTS

A senior civil servant challenged his transfer from the post of Chief Secretary, arguing that it was arbitrary and violated Articles 14 and 16.

02 / ISSUE

What does equality mean under Articles 14 and 16, and can arbitrary state action breach it?

03 / REASONING

The Court described equality as a dynamic concept and said arbitrariness is opposed to equality. Arbitrary state action therefore violates Article 14. The petition itself failed on its facts.

04 / TAKEAWAY

The “arbitrariness” test for Article 14. Later cases used it to strike down both executive actions and laws.

Mithu v. State of PunjabCriminal · (1983) 2 SCC 277＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1983) 2 SCC 277

01 / FACTS

A person convicted of murder while serving a life sentence faced Section 303 of the Indian Penal Code, which made the death sentence mandatory in such cases.

02 / ISSUE

Is a mandatory death sentence consistent with Articles 14 and 21?

03 / REASONING

The Court struck down Section 303. It held that a mandatory sentence that gives the judge no discretion to consider the circumstances is unfair and arbitrary.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Courts need discretion in sentencing. Check how the BNS now treats similar situations.

Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. DaniCriminal · (1978) 2 SCC 424＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1978) 2 SCC 424

01 / FACTS

A former Chief Minister was questioned by the police in a corruption case and was prosecuted after refusing to answer questions.

02 / ISSUE

How far does the right against self-incrimination in Article 20(3) apply during police investigation?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that the protection covers the stage of police questioning and can be claimed for answers that might incriminate. It said a person cannot refuse to answer every question, only those with that risk.

04 / TAKEAWAY

The right against self-incrimination applies at the investigation stage, not only in court.

Hussainara Khatoon v. State of BiharCriminal · (1980) 1 SCC 81＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1980) 1 SCC 81

01 / FACTS

Reports showed that very large numbers of undertrial prisoners in Bihar had been in jail for longer than the maximum sentence for their alleged offences.

02 / ISSUE

Does Article 21 include a right to a speedy trial and to free legal aid?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that a speedy trial is part of the fair procedure required by Article 21. It ordered the release of many undertrial prisoners and stressed free legal services for those who cannot afford a lawyer.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Speedy trial and free legal aid are part of Article 21, supported by Article 39A.

Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre v. State of MaharashtraCriminal · (2011) 1 SCC 694＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2011) 1 SCC 694

01 / FACTS

The accused sought anticipatory bail in a criminal case, and the High Court had refused it.

02 / ISSUE

What principles should guide a court deciding an anticipatory bail application, and how does it relate to personal liberty?

03 / REASONING

The Court stressed that Article 21 protects liberty and that anticipatory bail should not be refused on a rigid formula. It listed factors such as the nature of the accusation and the risk of the accused absconding, and said each case depends on its facts.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Anticipatory bail is closely tied to liberty and is decided on the facts. It is now dealt with in Section 482 of the BNSS.

Balfour v. BalfourContract · [1919] 2 KB 571＋

COURT OF APPEAL, ENGLAND · [1919] 2 KB 571

01 / FACTS

A husband promised to pay his wife a monthly allowance while he worked abroad. The couple later separated, and the wife sued for the payments.

02 / ISSUE

Was the arrangement a legally enforceable contract?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that it was not. Agreements between spouses living together are presumed not to be intended to create legal relations.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Intention to create legal relations is needed for a contract. The presumption for domestic arrangements can be rebutted in some situations.

Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur & Co.Contract · AIR 1954 SC 44＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · AIR 1954 SC 44

01 / FACTS

A company agreed to develop land and sell plots, but wartime requisition of the land caused delay. The buyer asked for his money back, saying the contract was frustrated.

02 / ISSUE

Was the contract discharged by frustration under Section 56 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that the doctrine of frustration in India is governed by Section 56, which covers supervening impossibility in a wider sense. On the facts, the delay was not enough to end the contract.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Section 56 is the Indian home of frustration. Mere hardship or delay is usually not enough, and the question is whether the foundation of the contract is destroyed.

Rylands v. FletcherTort · (1868) LR 3 HL 330＋

HOUSE OF LORDS · (1868) LR 3 HL 330

01 / FACTS

A mill owner built a reservoir on his land. Water escaped through old mine shafts and flooded a neighbour’s coal mine.

02 / ISSUE

Is a landowner liable for damage caused when a dangerous thing he brought onto his land escapes, even without negligence?

03 / REASONING

The House of Lords held that a person who brings onto his land and keeps something likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it at his peril, and is liable for the natural consequences of its escape.

04 / TAKEAWAY

The rule of strict liability. India went further with absolute liability in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India.

Salomon v. A. Salomon & Co. Ltd.Company · [1897] AC 22＋

HOUSE OF LORDS · [1897] AC 22

01 / FACTS

Salomon sold his business to a company he formed and held almost all its shares and debentures. The company failed, and creditors claimed his debts.

02 / ISSUE

Is a company separate from its members, or merely their agent, so that the controlling shareholder is personally liable?

03 / REASONING

The House of Lords held that a company properly incorporated is a separate legal person, even if one person owns nearly all of it.

04 / TAKEAWAY

The foundation of separate legal personality. Courts lift the corporate veil only in limited situations.

Foss v. HarbottleCompany · (1843) 2 Hare 461＋

COURT OF CHANCERY, ENGLAND · (1843) 2 Hare 461

01 / FACTS

Two shareholders sued the company’s directors for misusing company property.

02 / ISSUE

Can individual shareholders sue for a wrong done to the company itself?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that the company is the proper claimant for a wrong done to it. It would not interfere with internal irregularities that the majority could ratify.

04 / TAKEAWAY

The proper plaintiff rule and the majority rule. Exceptions exist, for example where there is fraud on the minority.

I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil NaduConstitutional · (2007) 2 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2007) 2 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Petitions challenged the constitutional immunity granted to laws placed in the Ninth Schedule under Article 31B after the Kesavananda Bharati judgment date.

02 / ISSUE

Can laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 be reviewed for violating fundamental rights and the basic structure?

03 / REASONING

A nine-judge Bench held that all constitutional amendments and laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review against the basic structure doctrine, including the essence of fundamental rights in Part III.

04 / TAKEAWAY

The Ninth Schedule is not a blanket shield against judicial review. Laws added after the Kesavananda judgment date must respect the basic structure.

Waman Rao v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (1981) 2 SCC 362＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1981) 2 SCC 362

01 / FACTS

The validity of the Maharashtra Agricultural Lands (Ceiling on Holdings) Act and Article 31B was challenged by landholders.

02 / ISSUE

Does the basic structure doctrine apply retrospectively to constitutional amendments made before the Kesavananda decision?

03 / REASONING

The Court drew a clear line at 24 April 1973. Amendments made before that date are treated as valid, while amendments made on or after that date are subject to the basic structure test.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Established 24 April 1973 as the permanent cut-off date for basic structure scrutiny of constitutional amendments.

I.C. Golaknath v. State of PunjabConstitutional · (1967) 2 SCR 762＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1967) 2 SCR 762

01 / FACTS

Family members challenged the Punjab Security of Land Tenures Act, 1953, claiming it violated their fundamental rights to property and equality.

02 / ISSUE

Can Parliament amend Part III of the Constitution so as to take away or abridge Fundamental Rights?

03 / REASONING

An 11-judge Bench held by a 6:5 majority that constitutional amendments are 'law' under Article 13(2), so Parliament had no power to abridge fundamental rights. The Court applied prospective overruling.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Historic milestone leading to the 24th Amendment and ultimately the Basic Structure Doctrine in Kesavananda Bharati.

Sajjan Singh v. State of RajasthanConstitutional · (1965) 1 SCR 933＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1965) 1 SCR 933

01 / FACTS

Landowners challenged the 17th Constitutional Amendment, which added multiple state land ceiling enactments to the Ninth Schedule.

02 / ISSUE

Did the 17th Amendment unconstitutionally restrict High Court writ jurisdiction and violate fundamental rights?

03 / REASONING

The Court upheld the amendment by majority, reaffirming Shankari Prasad. However, Justice Mudholkar and Justice Hidayatullah wrote pioneering doubts asking whether basic features of the Constitution could ever be amended.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Planted the intellectual seeds for the eventual emergence of the Basic Structure Doctrine.

S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (First Judges Case)Constitutional · 1981 Supp SCC 87＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · 1981 Supp SCC 87

01 / FACTS

Lawyers challenged the transfer of High Court judges and the non-appointment of additional judges without the Chief Justice's agreement.

02 / ISSUE

Does the word 'consultation' in Articles 124 and 217 mean concurrence of the Chief Justice of India, and do practicing lawyers have standing to sue?

03 / REASONING

A seven-judge Bench held that 'consultation' does not mean 'concurrence' and that executive opinion had primacy. Crucially, the Court liberalized locus standi, formally ushering in Public Interest Litigation (PIL).

04 / TAKEAWAY

Established broad standing rules for Public Interest Litigation, though its appointment primacy ruling was later overturned.

Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. Union of India (Second Judges Case)Constitutional · (1993) 4 SCC 441＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1993) 4 SCC 441

01 / FACTS

Petitions were filed to reconsider the S.P. Gupta ruling and restore the independence of the judiciary in judicial appointments.

02 / ISSUE

Does the Chief Justice of India have primacy in appointments to the Supreme Court and High Courts?

03 / REASONING

A nine-judge Bench overruled S.P. Gupta. It held that 'consultation' requires the CJI's agreement, formulated in consultation with the two senior-most colleagues, creating the Collegium system.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Instituted the judicial Collegium system to safeguard judicial independence from executive dominance.

Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 (Third Judges Case)Constitutional · (1998) 7 SCC 739＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1998) 7 SCC 739

01 / FACTS

The President of India made a reference under Article 143 seeking clarification on the consultation process for judicial appointments.

02 / ISSUE

What is the precise composition and process of the Collegium for Supreme Court and High Court appointments and transfers?

03 / REASONING

A nine-judge Bench reaffirmed the Second Judges decision and clarified that the CJI must consult a Collegium of the four senior-most Supreme Court judges for Supreme Court elevations and two senior-most judges for High Court transfers.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Set the definitive operational guidelines and expanded membership for the Supreme Court Collegium.

Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. Union of India (NJAC Case)Constitutional · (2016) 5 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2016) 5 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Parliament passed the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the NJAC Act, 2014, replacing the Collegium with a commission comprising judges, the Law Minister, and eminent persons.

02 / ISSUE

Did the 99th Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act damage the basic structure of judicial independence?

03 / REASONING

A five-judge Bench by 4:1 held that judicial primacy in appointments is an integral facet of judicial independence and part of the basic structure. The 99th Amendment and NJAC Act were declared void.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Judicial independence in appointments is non-negotiable basic structure; struck down the NJAC Act.

Romesh Thappar v. State of MadrasConstitutional · 1950 SCR 594＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · 1950 SCR 594

01 / FACTS

The Government of Madras banned the entry and circulation of the weekly journal 'Cross Roads' under the Maintenance of Public Order Act, 1949.

02 / ISSUE

Does freedom of speech and expression in Article 19(1)(a) include the freedom of propagation and circulation of ideas?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that freedom of speech includes freedom of circulation, for without circulation the publication would be of little value. Public safety restrictions did not fall within the narrow exceptions of Article 19(2) as then drafted.

04 / TAKEAWAY

A Hindu husband converted to Islam solely for the purpose of contracting a second marriage without obtaining a divorce from his first Hindu wife.

02 / ISSUE

Can a Hindu husband who converts to Islam marry a second time while his first marriage subsists, and is he guilty of bigamy under Section 494 IPC?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that a marriage solemnized under Hindu law can only be dissolved under that law. Religious conversion does not automatically dissolve the prior marriage, and contracting a second marriage constitutes bigamy under Section 494 IPC.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Religious conversion cannot be used as a subterfuge to contract bigamous marriages; second marriage without divorce is void and punishable.

Lily Thomas v. Union of India (Bigamy Case)Family · (2000) 6 SCC 224＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2000) 6 SCC 224

01 / FACTS

Petitions sought review of the Sarla Mudgal verdict, contending that prosecuting converted Muslim husbands infringed freedom of conscience under Article 25.

02 / ISSUE

Does prosecuting a person for bigamy who converts to Islam to remarry without divorce violate Article 25 of the Constitution?

03 / REASONING

The Court reaffirmed Sarla Mudgal, holding that Article 25 protects religious conscience but does not sanction fraudulent conversion to circumvent personal law obligations or commit bigamy.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Reaffirmed bigamy prosecution for husbands converting to Islam to remarry; religious freedom does not shelter matrimonial fraud.

B.P. Singhal v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2010) 6 SCC 331＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2010) 6 SCC 331

01 / FACTS

A newly elected Central Government removed Governors of four states without assigning reasons, prompting a challenge to arbitrary dismissal under Article 156(1).

02 / ISSUE

Can the President remove a state Governor at pleasure without assigning reasons or having compelling constitutional grounds?

03 / REASONING

A Constitution Bench held that while the President can remove a Governor at pleasure, the power cannot be exercised arbitrarily, capriciously, or based on political dislike. Removal must be founded on compelling reasons.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Governors cannot be removed arbitrarily merely due to a change of political party at the Centre.

S.R. Chaudhuri v. State of PunjabConstitutional · (2001) 7 SCC 126＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2001) 7 SCC 126

01 / FACTS

A non-legislator appointed as minister resigned at the end of the six-month grace period under Article 164(4) and was immediately reappointed without being elected.

02 / ISSUE

Can a non-legislator be repeatedly reappointed as a minister under Article 164(4) without getting elected to the legislative assembly?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that Article 164(4) is an exception intended for temporary exigencies. Reappointing a non-elected minister without election is a subversion of representative democracy and a fraud on the Constitution.

04 / TAKEAWAY

A non-elected person cannot be reappointed as a minister consecutively without winning an election within six months.

B.R. Kapur v. State of Tamil Nadu (Jayalalitha Case)Constitutional · (2001) 7 SCC 231＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2001) 7 SCC 231

01 / FACTS

J. Jayalalithaa was sworn in as Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu by the Governor despite having been convicted in a corruption case and disqualified from contesting elections.

02 / ISSUE

Can the Governor appoint a person as Chief Minister who is disqualified from being a member of the legislative assembly due to criminal conviction?

03 / REASONING

A Constitution Bench held that a person disqualified by conviction cannot be appointed Chief Minister under Article 164(4). The Governor's appointment was unconstitutional, and a writ of quo warranto was issued.

04 / TAKEAWAY

A person disqualified from being a legislator due to criminal conviction cannot be sworn in as Chief Minister.

Novartis AG v. Union of India (Glivec Case)IPR · (2013) 6 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2013) 6 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Swiss pharmaceutical giant Novartis sought an Indian patent for the beta-crystalline form of Imatinib Mesylate (cancer drug Glivec), which was rejected under Section 3(d).

02 / ISSUE

Does a new crystalline form of a known chemical substance constitute an invention under Section 3(d) of the Patents Act without proof of enhanced therapeutic efficacy?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that Section 3(d) was enacted to prevent 'evergreening' of patents. An applicant must demonstrate significantly enhanced therapeutic efficacy, not merely enhanced bioavailability or physical stability.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Prevented evergreening of pharmaceutical patents; Section 3(d) strictly requires demonstrated enhancement of therapeutic efficacy.

Bayer Corporation v. Union of India (Nexavar Case)IPR · (2014) SCC OnLine SC 1215＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2014) SCC OnLine SC 1215

01 / FACTS

Bayer challenged India’s first compulsory license granted to Natco Pharma under Section 84 of the Patents Act for producing an affordable generic of the kidney cancer drug Sorafenib (Nexavar).

02 / ISSUE

Was the grant of a compulsory license valid where the patentee priced the life-saving medicine at exorbitant rates and failed to manufacture locally?

03 / REASONING

The Supreme Court dismissed Bayer’s challenge, affirming that the patentee failed to make the drug available at a reasonably affordable price and failed to work the patent commercially in India on a sufficient scale.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Upheld India's compulsory licensing regime under Section 84 Patents Act to guarantee affordable access to life-saving medicines.

Eastern Book Company v. D.B. ModakIPR · (2008) 1 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2008) 1 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Publishers of Supreme Court Cases (SCC) sued competitors for copying copy-edited judgments, headnotes, paragraph numbering, and editorial footnotes.

02 / ISSUE

What standard of originality is required for copyright protection in law reports containing judicial judgments in the public domain?

03 / REASONING

The Court rejected the traditional English 'sweat of the brow' doctrine and adopted the Canadian 'modicum of creativity' standard. Mere copy-editing of text lacks copyright, but original headnotes and editorial arrangements are copyright-protected.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Adopted the 'modicum of creativity' test for copyright in India; judicial decisions are public domain, but editorial headnotes are protected.

R.G. Anand v. Delux FilmsIPR · (1978) 4 SCC 118＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1978) 4 SCC 118

01 / FACTS

The author of the play 'Hum Hindustani' alleged that the film 'New Delhi' was an actionable infringement of his copyright in the play’s provincial marriage theme.

02 / ISSUE

What is the boundary between an unprotected idea and protected expression under copyright law?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that there can be no copyright in an idea, subject matter, or historical theme. Copyright protection is confined to the specific expression and arrangement. Minor similarities on broad themes do not constitute infringement.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Locus classicus on the idea-expression dichotomy in copyright law; ideas are free as air, only the specific form of expression is protected.

Yahoo! Inc. v. Akash AroraIPR · 1999 (19) PTC 201 (Del)＋

DELHI HIGH COURT · 1999 (19) PTC 201 (Del)

01 / FACTS

The global search engine Yahoo! sought an injunction against an Indian defendant who registered and operated the domain name 'YahooIndia.com'.

02 / ISSUE

Does the law of passing off apply to internet domain names, and can cyber-squatting be restrained like trademark infringement?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that a domain name serves the same function as a trademark in cyberspace. Passing off principles apply with full force to misleadingly similar domain names that cause confusion.

04 / TAKEAWAY

First Indian cyber-law landmark extending trademark and passing-off protections to internet domain names.

State of Rajasthan v. VidyawatiTort · 1962 Supp (2) SCR 989＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · 1962 Supp (2) SCR 989

01 / FACTS

A government driver driving a government jeep back from repairs knocked down a pedestrian, killing him, and the state claimed sovereign immunity.

02 / ISSUE

Is the state immune from vicarious liability for the tortious acts of its employees under Article 300 of the Constitution?

03 / REASONING

The Constitution Bench held that the state is vicariously liable for torts committed by its servants in the course of employment, rejecting sovereign immunity for commercial or non-sovereign driving activities in a welfare republic.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Held the State vicariously liable in tort for the negligence of its servants in ordinary non-sovereign functions.

Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v. State of U.P.Tort · (1965) 1 SCR 375＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1965) 1 SCR 375

01 / FACTS

A merchant's gold was seized by police on suspicion of being stolen and placed in the malkhana, from where a constable stole it and fled to Pakistan.

02 / ISSUE

Can the state claim sovereign immunity when seized property is stolen due to the negligence of police officers exercising sovereign powers?

03 / REASONING

A Constitution Bench held that maintaining public order and arrest are sovereign functions, granting immunity to the state. However, the Court expressed grave dissatisfaction with the archaic rule, urging legislative reform.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Upheld sovereign immunity for police arrest and custody, an archaic doctrine severely restricted and bypassed by later constitutional tort cases.

Nilabati Behera v. State of OrissaTort · (1993) 2 SCC 746＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1993) 2 SCC 746

01 / FACTS

A 22-year-old youth taken into police custody was found dead the next morning on railway tracks with severe multiple lacerations.

02 / ISSUE

Can constitutional courts grant monetary compensation under public law for violation of fundamental rights caused by custodial violence?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that sovereign immunity has no application to public law remedies under Articles 32 and 226. Awarding monetary compensation for custodial death is an enforceable constitutional remedy for infringement of Article 21.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Consolidated public law damages for custodial death, stripping the state of sovereign immunity defences for constitutional torts.

Rudul Sah v. State of BiharTort · (1983) 4 SCC 141＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1983) 4 SCC 141

01 / FACTS

A man acquitted of murder in 1968 was illegally kept imprisoned in jail for 14 additional years after his acquittal until a habeas corpus petition was filed.

02 / ISSUE

Can the Supreme Court award monetary compensation under Article 32 for illegal and unconstitutional deprivation of personal liberty?

03 / REASONING

Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud held that constitutional courts have inherent power under Article 32 to grant monetary compensation as a palliative for grave violations of Article 21 to hold the state accountable.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Birth of constitutional compensatory jurisprudence in India; established that monetary compensation can be awarded under Article 32.

Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. GuptaConsumer · (1994) 1 SCC 243＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1994) 1 SCC 243

01 / FACTS

Homebuyers suffered prolonged delays and harassment by statutory development authorities in handing over promised residential flats.

02 / ISSUE

Do statutory development authorities fall within the definition of 'service' under the Consumer Protection Act, and can public officials be fined for harassment?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that housing construction by statutory authorities is a 'service'. It ruled that public officials who cause harassment through arbitrary delay can have compensation recovered from their personal salaries.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Brought statutory housing boards under the Consumer Protection Act and allowed personal recovery from delinquent public officials.

Indian Medical Association v. V.P. ShanthaConsumer · (1995) 6 SCC 651＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1995) 6 SCC 651

01 / FACTS

The medical profession challenged the jurisdiction of consumer forums, arguing that medical treatment is not a 'service' under the Consumer Protection Act, 1986.

02 / ISSUE

Does medical treatment rendered by medical practitioners and hospitals constitute a 'service' under Section 2(1)(o) of the Consumer Protection Act?

03 / REASONING

A three-judge Bench held that medical services rendered for consideration fall within the Consumer Protection Act, making doctors and private hospitals liable for medical negligence before consumer redressal commissions.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Landmark ruling bringing medical professionals and private healthcare providers under the Consumer Protection Act.

Spring Meadows Hospital v. Harjol AhluwaliaConsumer · (1998) 4 SCC 39＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1998) 4 SCC 39

01 / FACTS

An infant suffered permanent brain damage due to an unqualified nurse injecting an excessive adult dose of a medicine in a private hospital.

02 / ISSUE

Are parents who pay for their child's medical treatment 'consumers' entitled to compensation for mental agony and medical negligence?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that both the child beneficiary and the parents who hired the service are consumers under the Act, awarding substantial compensation for permanent medical damage.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Affirmed that parents and family members paying for pediatric medical treatment are protected consumers under consumer law.

R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu (Auto Shankar Case)Constitutional · (1994) 6 SCC 632＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1994) 6 SCC 632

01 / FACTS

Condemned prisoner Auto Shankar wrote an autobiography detailing ties with senior police and IAS officials. The state sought to enjoin a magazine from publishing it.

02 / ISSUE

Can the state or its officials impose prior restraint on the publication of memoirs under the guise of defamation or privacy?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that public officials have no right to privacy in respect of their public acts. No prior restraint can be imposed on publication; officials can only sue for damages if false statements are published with reckless disregard for truth.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Introduced the Sullivan standard to India; prohibited prior restraint on press publications regarding public officials' conduct.

Subramanian Swamy v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2016) 7 SCC 221＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2016) 7 SCC 221

01 / FACTS

Political leaders challenged the constitutional validity of Sections 499 and 500 IPC providing for criminal defamation.

02 / ISSUE

Does criminal defamation impose an unreasonable restriction on freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(2)?

03 / REASONING

The Court upheld the constitutional validity of criminal defamation, ruling that reputation is a facet of personal dignity protected under Article 21, which constitutes a reasonable restriction under Article 19(2).

04 / TAKEAWAY

Upheld the constitutionality of criminal defamation, balancing individual reputation under Article 21 with freedom of speech.

P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (CBI/SPE)Constitutional · (1998) 4 SCC 626＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1998) 4 SCC 626

01 / FACTS

MPs were accused of accepting bribes to vote against a no-confidence motion to save the minority government, claiming parliamentary immunity under Article 105(2).

02 / ISSUE

Does parliamentary immunity under Article 105(2) shield an MP from criminal prosecution for taking a bribe in respect of a vote in Parliament?

03 / REASONING

A 3:2 majority held that MPs who accepted bribes and cast their votes were immune from prosecution under Article 105(2). (Note: Overruled in 2024 by a 7-judge Bench in Sita Soren).

04 / TAKEAWAY

Historical majority ruling on parliamentary immunity, recently overruled to hold that bribery is not shielded by legislative privilege.
