CASE LIBRARY

19 judgments worth knowing.

Short learning summaries in the facts, issue, reasoning and takeaway format. Select a case to open it.

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (1978) 1 SCC 248＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1978) 1 SCC 248

01 / FACTS

The petitioner’s passport was impounded by the Government under the Passports Act, 1967, in the public interest, without prior reasons or a hearing.

02 / ISSUE

Must a law that restricts personal liberty provide a procedure that is fair, just and reasonable, and how do Articles 14, 19 and 21 relate to one another?

03 / REASONING

The Court read Article 21 together with Articles 14 and 19. A “procedure established by law” cannot be arbitrary, unfair or unreasonable, and a law affecting liberty must satisfy each Article that applies.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Fairness is part of Article 21, and arbitrary state action is open to challenge. How the principle works for a given statute still depends on its terms and the facts.

Shreya Singhal v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2015) 5 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2015) 5 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Petitions challenged Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, which punished certain online messages, after arrests over social media posts.

02 / ISSUE

Did Section 66A violate freedom of speech and expression, and was it saved by the reasonable restrictions in Article 19(2)?

03 / REASONING

The Court distinguished discussion and advocacy from incitement. It held Section 66A vague and overbroad, with a chilling effect on speech, and struck it down. It also read down the intermediary takedown rules and upheld Section 69A with safeguards.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Vague criminal laws on speech can be struck down. Online expression receives constitutional protection, subject to restrictions that fit within Article 19(2).

Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2017) 10 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2017) 10 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

A challenge to the Aadhaar scheme led to a reference to a nine-judge Bench on whether the Constitution guarantees a right to privacy.

02 / ISSUE

Is privacy a fundamental right under the Constitution of India?

03 / REASONING

The Bench held that privacy is intrinsic to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and to the freedoms in Part III. It overruled earlier decisions to the extent they said otherwise, and set out that any interference must meet tests of legality, legitimate aim and proportionality.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Privacy is a fundamental right. The decision does not decide every privacy dispute, and later cases apply its tests to specific laws.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of KeralaConstitutional · (1973) 4 SCC 225＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1973) 4 SCC 225

01 / FACTS

The head of a Kerala mutt challenged state land-reform laws and constitutional amendments that limited judicial review of property rights. A 13-judge Bench heard the case.

02 / ISSUE

How far can Parliament amend the Constitution under Article 368?

03 / REASONING

A majority held that Parliament has wide power to amend the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter its basic structure or framework.

04 / TAKEAWAY

The basic structure doctrine. The list of basic features has been developed case by case since.

Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (1980) 3 SCC 625＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1980) 3 SCC 625

01 / FACTS

The petitioners challenged parts of the Constitution (42nd Amendment) Act, 1976, which limited judicial review of amendments and gave Directive Principles priority over certain Fundamental Rights.

02 / ISSUE

Is Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution limited, and can it make that power unlimited by amendment?

03 / REASONING

The Court struck down the challenged clauses. It held that the limited nature of the amending power is itself part of the basic structure, and that the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is essential to the Constitution.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Parliament cannot give itself unlimited amending power. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles must be read in harmony.

S.R. Bommai v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (1994) 3 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1994) 3 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Several state governments were dismissed and President’s Rule imposed under Article 356. In Karnataka, the Chief Minister said he still had a majority in the Assembly.

02 / ISSUE

Is the President’s satisfaction under Article 356 open to judicial review, and on what grounds?

03 / REASONING

A nine-judge Bench held that a proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review. It said majority support should be tested on the floor of the House, and treated secularism as part of the basic structure.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Article 356 is a limited, reviewable power. Federalism and secularism are basic features of the Constitution.

Indra Sawhney v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · 1992 Supp (3) SCC 217＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · 1992 Supp (3) SCC 217

01 / FACTS

A 1990 government order reserved 27% of civil posts for socially and educationally backward classes, following the Mandal Commission report.

02 / ISSUE

Is reservation for backward classes valid under Articles 15(4) and 16(4), and what limits apply?

03 / REASONING

A nine-judge Bench upheld reservation for backward classes. It held that the “creamy layer” must be excluded and that reservation should ordinarily not exceed 50%.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Sets the basic framework for OBC reservation. Later amendments and cases have addressed other questions, so check the current law.

Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2018) 10 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2018) 10 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

Petitions challenged Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code to the extent it criminalised consensual sexual conduct between adults of the same sex.

02 / ISSUE

Does Section 377 violate Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 when applied to consensual adult conduct?

03 / REASONING

A five-judge Bench held it unconstitutional to that extent, relying on dignity, autonomy, equality and constitutional morality. It overruled the 2013 decision in Suresh Kumar Koushal.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Constitutional morality, not social morality, guides the Court. Consensual same-sex conduct between adults is not a crime.

Joseph Shine v. Union of IndiaConstitutional · (2019) 3 SCC 39＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2019) 3 SCC 39

01 / FACTS

A petition challenged Section 497 IPC (adultery) and Section 198(2) CrPC. The offence could be committed only by a man, and the woman could not be prosecuted.

02 / ISSUE

Is the adultery offence consistent with Articles 14, 15 and 21?

03 / REASONING

A five-judge Bench struck down the provisions. It held that they treated a married woman as her husband’s property and denied her autonomy and equal treatment.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Adultery is no longer a criminal offence. It can still be relevant in civil matters such as divorce.

Vishaka v. State of RajasthanConstitutional · (1997) 6 SCC 241＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1997) 6 SCC 241

01 / FACTS

After the gang rape of a social worker in Rajasthan, women’s groups filed a petition about the lack of legal protection against sexual harassment at the workplace.

02 / ISSUE

Can the Court lay down enforceable guidelines to protect working women when no legislation exists?

03 / REASONING

Relying on Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 and on international conventions, the Court issued binding guidelines to prevent and address workplace sexual harassment.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Courts can fill a legislative gap. The guidelines were later replaced by the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.

Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal CorporationConstitutional · (1985) 3 SCC 545＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1985) 3 SCC 545

01 / FACTS

Pavement and slum dwellers in Bombay challenged eviction from the places where they lived and worked.

02 / ISSUE

Is the right to livelihood part of the right to life under Article 21?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that the right to life includes the right to livelihood, because losing one’s livelihood can mean losing the means to live. It added that the right can be restricted by a fair procedure established by law.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Livelihood is protected by Article 21. Eviction must follow a fair procedure, but the case does not create a right to occupy public land.

Bachan Singh v. State of PunjabCriminal · (1980) 2 SCC 684＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1980) 2 SCC 684

01 / FACTS

The accused was convicted of murder and sentenced to death. The case raised the constitutional validity of the death penalty.

02 / ISSUE

Is the death penalty under Section 302 IPC and the related sentencing provision constitutional?

03 / REASONING

A Constitution Bench upheld the death penalty by a majority but held that it should be imposed only in the “rarest of rare” cases, after weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Life imprisonment is the rule and death the exception, decided case by case. Later cases keep refining how the test is applied.

Lalita Kumari v. Government of Uttar PradeshCriminal · (2014) 2 SCC 1＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2014) 2 SCC 1

01 / FACTS

A father said the police did not register an FIR when he reported the kidnapping of his minor daughter.

02 / ISSUE

Must the police register an FIR under Section 154 CrPC whenever information discloses a cognizable offence?

03 / REASONING

A Constitution Bench held that registration is mandatory when the information discloses a cognizable offence, and that no preliminary inquiry is permitted then. A short, time-bound preliminary inquiry is allowed in limited categories of cases.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Police cannot refuse to register an FIR where a cognizable offence is disclosed. Section 154 CrPC is now Section 173 of the BNSS, so check the current text.

Arnesh Kumar v. State of BiharCriminal · (2014) 8 SCC 273＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (2014) 8 SCC 273

01 / FACTS

A husband sought anticipatory bail in a case under Section 498A of the Indian Penal Code and the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961. The Court noted a pattern of routine arrests.

02 / ISSUE

Should police arrest automatically in offences punishable with imprisonment up to seven years, or must they first apply statutory conditions for arrest?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that arrest is not a matter of course. Police were directed to apply the arrest criteria under Section 41 of the Code of Criminal Procedure and record reasons, and magistrates were told to check those reasons before authorising detention.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Arrest needs recorded justification. The CrPC has since been replaced by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, so check the corresponding provisions now in force.

Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.Contract · [1893] 1 QB 256＋

COURT OF APPEAL, ENGLAND · [1893] 1 QB 256

01 / FACTS

A company advertised a £100 reward to anyone who caught influenza after using its smoke ball as directed, and said it had deposited £1,000 at a bank. Mrs Carlill used the ball as directed and caught influenza.

02 / ISSUE

Was there a binding contract, or was the advertisement a mere sales “puff”?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that the advertisement was an offer to the whole world, accepted by anyone who performed its conditions. The bank deposit showed serious intention, and notice of acceptance was not required.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Leading case on unilateral offers and acceptance by performance. Section 8 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 reflects the idea.

Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas GhoseContract · (1903) ILR 30 Cal 539＋

PRIVY COUNCIL · (1903) ILR 30 Cal 539

01 / FACTS

A minor mortgaged his property to a moneylender in return for a loan, and later sought to cancel the mortgage.

02 / ISSUE

Is a minor’s agreement void or voidable, and can the lender recover the money?

03 / REASONING

The Privy Council held that a minor’s agreement is void from the start under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, so there is nothing to ratify. The lender could not recover the money from the minor under the restitution provisions.

04 / TAKEAWAY

A minor’s agreement is void in India. The case is a standard reference on capacity to contract.

Hadley v. BaxendaleContract · (1854) 9 Exch 341＋

COURT OF EXCHEQUER, ENGLAND · (1854) 9 Exch 341

01 / FACTS

A mill’s crankshaft broke. A carrier delayed delivering it to the repairers, and the mill owners claimed the profits they lost while the mill stood idle.

02 / ISSUE

How remote can losses be before a party is no longer liable to pay damages for breach?

03 / REASONING

The Court held that damages cover losses arising naturally from the breach, or losses that both parties could reasonably have contemplated when making the contract. The carrier did not know the mill would stop without the shaft.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Sets the remoteness rule for contract damages. Section 73 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 follows a similar approach.

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (Oleum Gas Leak)Tort · (1987) 1 SCC 395＋

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA · (1987) 1 SCC 395

01 / FACTS

Oleum gas leaked from a fertiliser plant in Delhi, harming people nearby. The Court considered the liability of enterprises that carry on hazardous activities.

02 / ISSUE

What standard of liability applies to an enterprise engaged in an inherently dangerous activity?

03 / REASONING

The Court laid down a rule of absolute liability. Such an enterprise must compensate those harmed, without the exceptions available under the English rule in Rylands v. Fletcher, and compensation can be linked to the enterprise’s size and capacity.

04 / TAKEAWAY

India applies absolute liability for hazardous industries. It is a key case on environmental and industrial accidents.

Donoghue v. StevensonTort · [1932] AC 562＋

HOUSE OF LORDS · [1932] AC 562

01 / FACTS

Mrs Donoghue drank ginger beer from an opaque bottle that allegedly contained a decomposed snail. A friend had bought it, so she had no contract with the manufacturer.

02 / ISSUE

Does a manufacturer owe a duty of care to a consumer with whom it has no contract?

03 / REASONING

By a majority, the House of Lords held that it does. Lord Atkin’s “neighbour principle” says one must take reasonable care to avoid acts that could foreseeably injure people closely affected by them.

04 / TAKEAWAY

Foundation of the modern law of negligence. Indian courts have referred to it when developing tort law.
