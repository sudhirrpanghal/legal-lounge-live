How to write a legal article

A step-by-step guide to choosing a topic, structuring your argument, citing sources and finishing a legal article.

← Back to home

A legal article takes one legal issue, examines the law on it, and argues for a position. It is shorter and more focused than a research paper and is usually 1,500 to 2,500 words. This guide shows how to go from a blank page to a finished, well-cited article.

Plan faster: use our article and paper planner to turn your topic and word count into a section-by-section outline.

1. Choose a topic you can argue

  • Narrow it. “Bail law” is too wide. “Whether Section 482 of the BNSS gives enough protection against arrest for people who have not been charged” is something you can examine in 2,000 words.
  • Make it current. A recent judgment, a new law or a gap in how a law works gives your article a reason to exist.
  • Test it. Can you finish this sentence: “This article argues that …”? If not, the topic is still a subject, not a thesis.
  • Check your sources. Make sure there are enough judgments, statutes or reports to rely on before you commit.

2. Research before you write

  1. Start with primary sources: the Constitution, the Act or Code, rules and notifications, and the full text of judgments.
  2. Then read secondary sources: journal articles, books, Law Commission and parliamentary committee reports.
  3. Keep a source log as you go. Write down the full citation and page or paragraph for each point.
  4. Check the subsequent history of every case: was it followed, distinguished, overruled or referred to a larger bench?
  5. Check that every statute section is the current, amended text.

See the full legal research guide.

3. Decide your thesis and outline

Your thesis is the position the article will defend. It should be specific and arguable, not a statement of what the law already says. A common structure is below. Adapt it to your topic.

SectionWhat it doesShare of words
TitleNames the issue and the angle-
IntroductionHook, background, thesis, roadmapabout 10%
Legal and conceptual frameworkThe statutes, rules and principles that matterabout 15%
Judicial developmentsThe key cases and what they decidedabout 20%
AnalysisYour reasoning on the issue, in sub-headingsabout 30%
Critical evaluationIs the law adequate? Gaps and difficultiesabout 10%
RecommendationsPractical suggestions for reformabout 8%
ConclusionFindings and final positionabout 7%

These shares are a common starting point, not a rule.

4. Write each section

Title

Be specific. A good pattern is “Main issue: angle or jurisdiction”. Aim for under about fifteen words.

Introduction

  • Hook: a recent case, a legal change or a pointed question.
  • Background: just enough for the reader to follow.
  • Thesis: one or two clear sentences.
  • Roadmap: a sentence on what each section does.

Keep it short. Save the detailed analysis for the body.

Legal and conceptual framework

Explain only the provisions and principles that your argument needs. Define technical terms the first time you use them.

Judicial developments

For each important case, say the name, the court and year, the key facts, the question, the holding and the principle, and then say why it matters for your issue. Do not just list cases.

Analysis

This is the core. Use sub-headings. Show how courts have read the law, where they disagree, where the law is unclear or hard to apply, and how technology or practice has changed things. Compare with other systems if it helps.

Critical evaluation and recommendations

Ask whether the law works. Any criticism or suggestion should rest on legal reasoning and reliable sources. Recommendations should be practical: an amendment, a rule, a court clarification, a better enforcement step.

Conclusion

Restate the central concern, summarise your main points, and state your position. Do not introduce a new argument.

5. Write in a clear academic style

  • Use the third person and phrases such as “This article argues” or “It is submitted that”.
  • Prefer the active voice: “The Court held”, not “It was held by the Court”.
  • Keep sentences short, with one main idea in each paragraph.
  • Start paragraphs with a topic sentence and end sections with a link to the next.
  • Avoid contractions, slang and padding. Say less, but say it with authority.

6. Cite as you write

Choose one citation style and use it throughout. Put the citation in a footnote at the exact point where you rely on a source, and give a pinpoint (a page or paragraph). Read our citation guide and try the citation generator.

7. Originality, plagiarism and AI

  • Write in your own words and give credit for every idea, fact or quotation you take.
  • Follow the plagiarism and similarity rules of the place you submit to.
  • Never invent or guess a case, statute or quotation. Check each citation in an authentic source such as the official court website, India Code or a recognised database.
  • If you use AI tools for any part of your work, follow the publisher’s policy and disclose it as required. An AI tool is not a source and cannot be cited as authority.

8. Sample outline (use as a pattern)

Title: Anticipatory bail and personal liberty: how far does Section 482 of the BNSS protect it?
1. Introduction: a recent bail dispute, the problem, the thesis, the roadmap.
2. Legal framework: Section 482 BNSS, Article 21, and the older Section 438 CrPC.
3. Judicial developments: two or three leading cases on anticipatory bail and what each held.
4. Analysis: (a) when courts grant it, (b) conditions and duration, (c) practical difficulties.
5. Critical evaluation: is the protection effective for people who fear arrest?
6. Recommendations: clearer guidelines, faster hearing, better record-keeping.
7. Conclusion: restate the thesis and the way forward.

9. Checklist before you submit

  • The title is specific and matches the content.
  • The thesis is clear and arguable.
  • The introduction is short and ends with a roadmap.
  • Every legal statement has a footnote.
  • I verified every case, section and quotation against the original source.
  • I checked whether each case is still good law.
  • Cases are analysed, not just listed.
  • The analysis shows my own reasoning.
  • Recommendations are practical and linked to the analysis.
  • The conclusion adds no new argument.
  • One citation style is used consistently.
  • Word count, font, spacing and heading format follow the rules I was given.
  • I proofread twice and ran a similarity check where required.
  • I disclosed any AI use as the rules require.

10. Common mistakes

  • A topic that is too broad.
  • Describing the law instead of analysing it.
  • Listing cases without explaining their relevance.
  • Citing from memory or from a secondary summary instead of the source.
  • Mixing citation styles.
  • A conclusion that repeats the introduction or adds new points.
  • Leaving citations to the end.
