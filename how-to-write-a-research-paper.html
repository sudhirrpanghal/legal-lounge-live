How to write a legal research paper

Research question, literature review, methodology, analysis and citations: how to structure a legal research paper.

← Back to home

A legal research paper is a longer, more formal piece of scholarship, often 3,500 to 4,000 words or more. It asks a research question, explains how the question will be answered, reviews the existing literature and gives a reasoned answer. This guide covers each part.

1. Title, abstract and keywords

  • Title: specific and informative. It should signal the topic, the legal framework or the issue.
  • Abstract: usually 150 to 200 words. Cover the objective, the method, the main findings and the conclusion. Write it last.
  • Keywords: usually four to six terms that capture the central themes, for example “anticipatory bail”, “personal liberty”, “criminal procedure”. They help readers find the paper.

2. Introduction

Set the context and show why the topic matters now. Then state the research problem, the research question, the objectives, the scope and a short overview of how the paper is organised. Keep detailed analysis for later.

Weak research questionStronger research question
What is anticipatory bail?To what extent do courts’ conditions on anticipatory bail under the BNSS protect personal liberty while allowing investigation?
Is the law on cybercrime good?Does the electronic-evidence regime in Section 63 of the BSA give courts a workable test for authenticity?

3. Literature review

Survey what judges, legislators, regulators and scholars have already said. Organise it by theme or debate, not as a list of summaries. Critically assess it, and point out gaps or conflicting views. Then say how your paper responds to the gap.

  • Judicial decisions and statutes.
  • Books and journal articles.
  • Reports by Law Commissions, committees and regulators.

4. Methodology

Explain how you did the research and why that method suits your question.

  • Doctrinal: analysing statutes, cases and legal principles. This is the most common method in law.
  • Comparative: comparing Indian law with another system or with international standards.
  • Empirical: using data, surveys or interviews to support legal arguments.
  • Socio-legal: studying how law works in society.

Also state the limits of your research, such as the period covered or the sources you could access.

5. Main analysis

This is the longest section. Break it into sub-headings that follow your research question. In each, set out the legal principle, analyse how it has been interpreted, and explain what follows from it. Use examples or case studies. Link every sub-section back to your question. Do not simply describe: argue.

6. Discussion

Step back and interpret the analysis. What does it mean for policy or practice? What are the strengths and weaknesses of the present law? What reforms or solutions are possible, and what are their limits?

7. Conclusion

Summarise the findings and answer the research question directly. Restate why the work matters and offer a final recommendation. Do not add new arguments.

8. A typical word budget

For a paper of about 3,750 words, the main parts often look like this. The abstract and keywords are usually extra.

PartApproximate words

9. Citations and formatting

  • Use footnotes at the bottom of the page where the reference appears, numbered in sequence from 1.
  • Use one citation style throughout. See the citation guide.
  • Typical formatting is a standard serif font, 12 point, double spacing and one-inch margins, with page numbers. Your journal or competition may ask for something different.
  • Use clear headings and sub-headings.

10. Ethics, originality and AI

  • Plagiarism is unacceptable. Cite every source, and check the similarity limit set by your publisher.
  • Never create or cite a source you have not read and verified.
  • Follow the publisher’s rules on AI tools, and disclose any use as required.

11. Submission and peer review

  1. Read the call for papers and the author guidelines first.
  2. Check word count, formatting and citation style.
  3. Prepare the abstract, keywords and any declarations needed.
  4. If reviewers ask for changes, respond point by point and show where you changed the text.
  5. Keep a copy of every version.

Checklist

  • Title, abstract and keywords are done.
  • The research question is clear and answerable.
  • The literature review is organised by theme and shows a gap.
  • The methodology is explained and justified.
  • The analysis argues, and does not merely describe.
  • The discussion covers implications and reform.
  • The conclusion answers the question and adds nothing new.
  • Every source is verified and cited in one style.
  • Formatting, word count and declarations follow the rules.
