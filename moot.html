Moot court corner

How to read a moot problem, frame issues, draft a memorial and argue before a bench.

← Back to home

What a moot court is

A moot court is a simulated court hearing built around a fictional case, called a moot problem. Two sides argue before a bench of judges, usually in writing first (the memorial) and then orally. It trains research, drafting, structure and thinking on your feet.

Step 1: read the moot problem like a lawyer

  • Make a timeline of the facts and note who the parties are and which court is hearing the matter.
  • Separate facts you know from facts you are assuming. Never add facts that are not in the problem.
  • Underline the legal questions the facts raise, then list the laws and cases that might apply.
  • Read the rules of the competition carefully: word limits, formatting, citation style and the form of address for the bench.

Step 2: frame the issues

An issue is a precise legal question. A good issue names the law and the key fact. Frame it neutrally as a question, then build your side’s answer.

Weak: “Is the detention bad?”
Better: “Whether the detention of the petitioner under a general preventive order, without communicating the grounds, violated Articles 21 and 22 of the Constitution of India?”

Step 3: build your arguments

  • Group authorities by issue: the Constitution or statute first, then binding precedent, then persuasive sources.
  • For each case you cite, know the facts, the ratio and the court. Be ready to distinguish cases that go against you.
  • Use one clear structure for each argument: claim, law, application to the facts, conclusion.
  • Prepare the strongest argument for the other side and your answer to it.

Typical parts of a memorial

Rules differ between competitions, so always follow the official format. A common structure is:

  1. Cover page
  2. Table of contents
  3. Index of authorities
  4. Statement of jurisdiction
  5. Statement of facts
  6. Statement of issues
  7. Summary of arguments
  8. Arguments advanced (issue by issue)
  9. Prayer

Oral advocacy tips

  • Open with a roadmap: introduce yourself, your side, and the order of your submissions.
  • Answer the bench first: if a judge asks a question, answer it directly, then return to your structure.
  • Keep to your time: practise with a timer and know which points to drop if you run short.
  • Be honest about facts and cases: never misstate a holding. Admit what you do not know and offer to address it.
  • Keep the prayer ready: end by stating exactly what relief you ask the Court to grant.

Common mistakes

  • Citing a case without knowing its facts or whether it was overruled.
  • Arguing facts that are not in the problem.
  • Reading from the memorial instead of speaking to the bench.
  • Ignoring the other side’s strongest point.
  • Using old section numbers. Check whether the BNS, BNSS or BSA applies to your problem, and see our section finder.

Practice exercise: frame the issues

Fictional facts: Riya, a law student, joins a peaceful protest outside a district office against a local ordinance. The police detain her for several hours under a general preventive order and do not tell her the grounds. She is released the same night and files a petition.
