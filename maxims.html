Criminal law

Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit reaCriminal law＋

Meaning: An act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty.
Example: A person who takes an identical bag from a stand, honestly believing it is theirs, lacks the dishonest intention that theft requires.

Actus reusCriminal law＋

Meaning: The guilty act: the physical or conduct element of an offence.
Example: Firing a gun at another person is the actus reus of attempting to cause death, but liability also depends on the mental element.

Mens reaCriminal law＋

Meaning: The guilty mind: the mental element required for an offence.
Example: Causing a death by accident and causing it intending to kill are treated very differently because the mental states differ.

Ignorantia juris non excusatCriminal law＋

Meaning: Ignorance of the law is no excuse.
Example: A trader cannot escape a penalty by saying he did not know that a licence was required.

Ignorantia facti excusatCriminal law＋

Meaning: Ignorance (mistake) of fact can excuse.
Example: A guest who leaves with a coat identical to his own, honestly believing it is his, has made a mistake of fact.

Nullum crimen sine legeCriminal law＋

Meaning: No crime without law.
Example: A person cannot be punished for conduct that was not an offence when it was done. Article 20(1) of the Constitution bars retrospective criminal laws.

Ex post factoCriminal law＋

Meaning: After the fact: a law that is applied backwards.
Example: A law that makes an act an offence from a date earlier than the law itself cannot be used to convict a person, because of Article 20(1).

Nemo bis punitur pro eodem delictoCriminal law＋

Meaning: No one is punished twice for the same offence.
Example: Article 20(2) protects a person who has been prosecuted and punished for an offence from being prosecuted and punished for it again.

Nemo tenetur seipsum accusareCriminal law＋

Meaning: No one is bound to accuse himself.
Example: Article 20(3) says a person accused of an offence cannot be compelled to be a witness against himself.

In dubio pro reoCriminal law＋

Meaning: In doubt, favour the accused.
Example: If the evidence leaves a reasonable doubt about guilt, the accused gets the benefit of that doubt.

Doli incapaxCriminal law＋

Meaning: Incapable of crime.
Example: A child under seven years of age cannot commit an offence, as Section 20 of the BNS provides.

Corpus delictiCriminal law＋

Meaning: The body of the offence: proof that the crime was actually committed.
Example: To prove murder, the prosecution must show that a death occurred and was caused by a criminal act, as well as who did it.

Mala in seCriminal law＋

Meaning: Wrongs in themselves.
Example: Murder is wrong by its very nature, whether or not a statute forbids it.

Mala prohibitaCriminal law＋

Meaning: Wrongs because they are prohibited.
Example: Parking in a no-parking zone is wrong only because a rule forbids it.

Falsus in uno, falsus in omnibusCriminal law＋

Meaning: False in one thing, false in everything.
Example: Indian courts do not apply this as a rigid rule. They separate the reliable part of a witness’s account from the unreliable part.

Tort law

Volenti non fit injuriaTort law＋

Meaning: No injury is done to one who consents.
Example: A person who joins a boxing match accepts the ordinary risks of being hit, but not deliberate foul play.

Damnum sine injuriaTort law＋

Meaning: Damage without a legal injury: loss that does not violate a legal right gives no claim.
Example: A shop that loses customers to a new rival lawfully opening across the road cannot sue for the loss.

Injuria sine damnoTort law＋

Meaning: A legal injury without damage: violating a right can be actionable even without loss.
Example: Trespassing on someone’s land can be actionable even if no harm is done to the land.

Res ipsa loquiturTort law＋

Meaning: The thing speaks for itself.
Example: If a surgical instrument is left inside a patient after an operation, the circumstances themselves suggest negligence.

Ubi jus ibi remediumTort law＋

Meaning: Where there is a right, there is a remedy.
Example: If a person’s legal right is violated, the law should give a remedy such as damages or an injunction.

Sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedasTort law＋

Meaning: Use your own property so as not to harm another’s.
Example: A factory should not let toxic fumes drift onto neighbouring homes.

Respondeat superiorTort law＋

Meaning: Let the superior answer.
Example: A company can be liable when its driver, acting in the course of employment, negligently injures a pedestrian.

Qui facit per alium facit per seTort law＋

Meaning: He who acts through another acts himself.
Example: A principal can be bound by what an agent does within the authority given.

Actio personalis moritur cum personaTort law＋

Meaning: A personal action dies with the person.
Example: Under the traditional rule, some personal claims such as defamation did not survive the death of the person wronged. Statutes now change this in many situations.

Causa proxima, non remota spectaturTort law＋

Meaning: The immediate cause, not the remote cause, is considered.
Example: If a later, independent event breaks the chain, an earlier negligent act may not be treated as the cause of the harm.

Contract and property

Pacta sunt servandaContract and property＋

Meaning: Agreements must be kept.
Example: A party who signs a valid contract is bound to perform it or pay damages.

Caveat emptorContract and property＋

Meaning: Let the buyer beware.
Example: A buyer who inspects and buys a second-hand item as it is generally cannot complain about defects that were visible, subject to consumer and sale of goods laws.

Ex nudo pacto non oritur actioContract and property＋

Meaning: No action arises from a bare agreement.
Example: A promise to give a gift, with nothing given in return, is generally not enforceable as a contract, subject to the exceptions in Section 25 of the Indian Contract Act.

Ex turpi causa non oritur actioContract and property＋

Meaning: No action arises from an immoral or illegal cause.
Example: A person cannot sue to enforce an agreement to commit a crime.

Consensus ad idemContract and property＋

Meaning: Agreement on the same thing.
Example: If one party offers to sell one plot and the other thinks he is buying a different plot, there may be no real agreement.

Nemo dat quod non habetContract and property＋

Meaning: No one gives what he does not have.
Example: A thief cannot give a buyer good title to a stolen bicycle, subject to limited statutory exceptions.

Quid pro quoContract and property＋

Meaning: Something for something.
Example: A promise to pay a price in return for a promise to deliver goods is supported by consideration.

Quantum meruitContract and property＋

Meaning: As much as is earned.
Example: A contractor who is stopped partway through a job, without his fault, may claim reasonable payment for the work done.

Verba chartarum fortius accipiuntur contra proferentemContract and property＋

Meaning: The words of a document are taken most strongly against the person who put them forward.
Example: If a term in a standard-form contract is unclear, the court may read it against the party who drafted it.

Quod ab initio non valet, in tractu temporis non convalescitContract and property＋

Meaning: What is invalid from the start does not become valid with time.
Example: An agreement that was void when made does not become valid just because years pass.

Nemo potest facere per alium quod per se non potestContract and property＋

Meaning: No one can do through another what he cannot do himself.
Example: A person barred by law from doing an act cannot get around the bar by using an agent.

Pendente lite nihil innoveturContract and property＋

Meaning: While the suit is pending, nothing new should be introduced.
Example: Under Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act, a transfer of the disputed property during the suit is subject to the outcome of the suit.

Falsa demonstratio non nocetContract and property＋

Meaning: A false description does not harm.
Example: If a deed gives the wrong number for a house but identifies it clearly in other ways, the intended house passes.

Non est factumContract and property＋

Meaning: It is not my deed.
Example: A person tricked into signing a document that is fundamentally different from what they thought they signed may plead that it is not their deed.

In pari delicto potior est conditio possidentisContract and property＋

Meaning: Where both are equally at fault, the possessor’s position is stronger.
Example: If two parties are equally at fault in an illegal scheme, the court generally leaves the property where it lies.

Qui prior est tempore potior est jureContract and property＋

Meaning: He who is first in time is stronger in right.
Example: Where two equal interests compete, the earlier one generally has priority.

Quicquid plantatur solo, solo ceditContract and property＋

Meaning: Whatever is fixed to the soil belongs to the soil.
Example: A building permanently attached to land generally goes with the land when the land is sold.

Evidence and procedure

Res judicataEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: A matter already decided.
Example: Section 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure stops the same parties from litigating again a matter that has been finally decided between them.

Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litiumEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: It is in the public interest that litigation should end.
Example: This idea supports res judicata and the rules on limitation.

Actori incumbit onus probandiEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: The burden of proof lies on the claimant.
Example: A person who sues for money lent generally has to prove the loan.

Lex non requirit verificari quod apparet curiaeEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: The law does not require proof of what is apparent to the court.
Example: A court can take judicial notice of well-known facts, such as the date of a national holiday, without evidence.

Res gestaeEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Things done.
Example: A statement made at the time of an event and so closely tied to it that it forms part of the event may be relevant as part of the same transaction.

Allegans contraria non est audiendusEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: A person who makes contradictory statements is not to be heard.
Example: A party cannot take one position in a pleading and the opposite position at trial without explanation.

Qui tacet consentire videturEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: He who is silent appears to consent.
Example: Silence can amount to acceptance in some situations, but not as a general rule, because it depends on whether there was a duty to speak.

Sub judiceEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Under judicial consideration.
Example: Public comment that could prejudice a trial still going on may raise contempt concerns.

Ex parteEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: From one side only.
Example: A court may pass an interim order ex parte in an urgent case, and the other side can later apply to set it aside.

Locus standiEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Standing: the right to bring a case.
Example: A person with no connection to the matter generally cannot sue, but courts have relaxed this rule in public interest litigation.

In cameraEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: In private.
Example: Some sensitive proceedings are heard in camera to protect the privacy of the people involved.

Amicus curiaeEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Friend of the court.
Example: A court may appoint an advocate as amicus curiae to assist it on a difficult question of law.

Coram non judiceEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Before one who is not a judge.
Example: A decision made by a court that had no jurisdiction can be treated as a nullity.

Prima facieEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: On first appearance.
Example: A complaint that discloses the elements of an offence on its face makes out a prima facie case.

Per incuriamEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Through want of care.
Example: A decision given without noticing a binding statute or precedent has reduced value as a precedent.

Obiter dictaEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Things said in passing.
Example: A judge’s remark on a point that was not needed to decide the case is obiter. It may persuade, but it does not bind.

Ratio decidendiEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: The reason for the decision.
Example: The principle of law necessary to the decision is the part that binds later courts.

Stare decisisEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: To stand by decided matters.
Example: Under Article 141, the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts in India.

Functus officioEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Having discharged the office.
Example: Once a court has finally decided a matter and signed its order, it generally cannot change it except as the law allows, for example to correct a clerical error.

Suo motuEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: On its own motion.
Example: The Supreme Court can take up a matter suo motu on the basis of a letter or a news report.

Sine dieEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Without a day.
Example: A hearing adjourned sine die is postponed with no new date fixed.

Nunc pro tuncEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Now for then.
Example: A court may correct a record by an order that takes effect as of an earlier date.

Inter aliaEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: Among other things.
Example: The petition challenges the order, inter alia, on the ground that it was made without notice.

Ipso factoEvidence and procedure＋

Meaning: By that very fact.
Example: If a law says a convicted person is disqualified from an office, the disqualification may follow ipso facto, without a separate order.

Natural justice and public law

Audi alteram partemNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: Hear the other side.
Example: An employee cannot be dismissed for misconduct without being told the charge and given a chance to reply.

Nemo judex in causa suaNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: No one should be a judge in his own cause.
Example: A judge with a financial interest in a company should not hear a case involving that company.

Judex non potest esse testis in propria causaNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: A judge cannot be a witness in his own cause.
Example: A judge who personally saw the event in dispute should not both decide the case and act as its witness.

Lex dilationes abhorretNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: The law abhors delay.
Example: Courts discourage repeated adjournments and expect cases to be decided without avoidable delay.

Fiat justitia ruat caelumNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: Let justice be done though the heavens fall.
Example: It is quoted to stress that justice must be done even when the result is uncomfortable.

Salus populi est suprema lexNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: The welfare of the people is the supreme law.
Example: It is used to justify reasonable restrictions on rights in the public interest, within the limits of the Constitution.

Ultra viresNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: Beyond the powers.
Example: A rule that goes beyond what its parent Act allows can be struck down as ultra vires.

Delegata potestas non potest delegariNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: A delegated power cannot be delegated again.
Example: An officer given a power by a statute cannot hand it to someone else unless the law allows it.

Lex non cogit ad impossibiliaNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: The law does not compel the impossible.
Example: A person cannot be punished for failing to do something that was impossible.

Impotentia excusat legemNatural justice and public law＋

Meaning: Impossibility excuses non-compliance with the law.
Example: Failure to obey an order may be excused if compliance was truly impossible.

Interpretation of statutes

Ejusdem generisInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: Of the same kind.
Example: In “cars, buses, trucks and other vehicles”, the last words may be read to cover only motor vehicles of that kind.

Noscitur a sociisInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: A word is known by the company it keeps.
Example: An unclear word in a list is understood from the words around it.

Expressio unius est exclusio alteriusInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: The express mention of one thing excludes others.
Example: If a statute lists the places where a power can be used, other places are presumed to be left out.

Generalia specialibus non derogantInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: General provisions do not override special ones.
Example: If a special law deals with a specific matter, a general law does not displace it.

Lex posterior derogat legi prioriInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: A later law overrides an earlier one.
Example: If two laws of equal rank conflict, the later one generally prevails.

Lex prospicit non respicitInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: The law looks forward, not backward.
Example: A new law is presumed to apply from now on, unless it clearly says otherwise.

Interpretatio fienda est ut res magis valeat quam pereatInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: A construction should be preferred that gives effect to a provision rather than defeats it.
Example: A court prefers a reading of a clause that makes it work over a reading that makes it meaningless.

Contemporanea expositio est optima et fortissima in legeInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: Contemporary interpretation is the best and strongest in law.
Example: How an old statute was understood and applied when it was passed can help in reading it.

Ubi eadem ratio ibi idem jusInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: Where there is the same reason, there is the same law.
Example: A principle applied in one case is applied to another case that rests on the same reason.

Cessante ratione legis cessat ipsa lexInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: When the reason for a law ends, the law itself ends.
Example: It is cited when a rule outlives the purpose for which it was made, though courts apply it carefully.

Mutatis mutandisInterpretation of statutes＋

Meaning: With the necessary changes.
Example: A rule that applies to one office applies mutatis mutandis to another office.

Writs

Habeas corpusWrits＋

Meaning: You may have the body.
Example: A petition asking the court to have a person who is allegedly illegally detained brought before it.

MandamusWrits＋

Meaning: We command.
Example: A writ directing a public authority to perform a public duty that it has failed to perform.

CertiorariWrits＋

Meaning: To be certified or informed.
Example: Used to quash an order of a lower court or tribunal that acted without jurisdiction or with a clear legal error.

Quo warrantoWrits＋

Meaning: By what authority.
Example: It challenges a person’s right to hold a public office.

General principles

Vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniuntGeneral principles＋

Meaning: The law helps those who are vigilant, not those who sleep over their rights.
Example: A claim brought long after the limitation period may be barred, even if it was once valid.

De minimis non curat lexGeneral principles＋

Meaning: The law does not concern itself with trifles.
Example: A claim for an extremely small, harmless loss may be dismissed as not worth the court’s time.

Ubi non est principalis non potest esse accessoriusGeneral principles＋

Meaning: Where there is no principal, there can be no accessory.
Example: This is an old rule of criminal liability. Modern provisions on abetment, such as Section 45 of the BNS, are wider, so check the statute.

Accessorium non ducit sed sequitur suum principaleGeneral principles＋

Meaning: An accessory follows its principal.
Example: A security such as a mortgage follows the debt it secures when the debt is transferred.

Aequitas sequitur legemGeneral principles＋

Meaning: Equity follows the law.
Example: Courts apply equitable principles in line with the legal rules and step in where the law alone would give an unfair result.

Bona fideGeneral principles＋

Meaning: In good faith.
Example: A buyer who pays full value without knowing of any defect in the seller’s title is a bona fide purchaser.

Mala fideGeneral principles＋

Meaning: In bad faith.
Example: An order passed to harass an employee, rather than for a genuine reason, can be challenged as mala fide.

Ab initioGeneral principles＋

Meaning: From the beginning.
Example: A contract with an unlawful object is void ab initio.

Pari passuGeneral principles＋

Meaning: On an equal footing.
Example: Creditors of the same class are paid pari passu from the available funds, in proportion to their claims.
